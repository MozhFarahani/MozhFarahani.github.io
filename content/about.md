I’m a scientist at the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR), where I work at the bridge between machine learning and the Earth system models used to understand and predict land, water, and climate.

Physics-based models capture how the world works, but they are expensive to run and hard to tune. Much of my work uses machine learning to make them faster and more accurate. I built ML emulators that calibrate a national hydrology model across hundreds of U.S. watersheds, scaled that approach to about 97,000 river basins for a multi-agency climate projection effort, and ran continental simulations testing how irrigation, land cover, and vegetation shape streamflow. I care as much about the engineering as the science, so the work ends up as open-source code, tested pipelines on supercomputers, and dashboards that partner teams actually use.

I also bring a background in information theory, the mathematics of how much one variable tells you about another. I use it to test whether a model, physical or machine learning, has learned the right relationships from its inputs, not just good scores.

So far, machine learning in my work has supported physics-based models from the outside, helping to calibrate them. Next, I want to bring the two closer together in hybrid and physics-informed models, where machine learning and physics learn together, and to use information theory to understand what these models learn and why.

Outside of work, I paint, play volleyball, and still follow the basketball which I used to play.
